\chapter{Introduction}\label{cap:intro}

The Internet of Things (IoT) connects billions of embedded devices --- smart cameras, industrial sensors, medical monitors, and home appliances --- into a single, globally addressable network. The number of IoT devices worldwide is forecast to more than double from 19.8 billion in 2025 to over 40.6 billion by 2034 \cite{Statista2025}, an exponential growth that has produced a vast and multifaceted attack surface. The financial ramifications are staggering: global damages from cybercrime are projected to reach USD 12.2 trillion annually by 2031 \cite{Morgan2025}. Malicious actors increasingly exploit insecure devices to steal data, disrupt services, or form large-scale botnets for Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks \cite{Neto2023,Tawalbeh2020}.

Securing this landscape presents unique and formidable challenges that render traditional Information Technology (IT) security paradigms insufficient. First, most IoT devices are severely resource-constrained, possessing minimal computational power, memory, and energy, which precludes the use of intensive security software like traditional firewalls or antivirus agents \cite{Chen2021}. Second, IoT ecosystems are defined by extreme heterogeneity, comprising countless devices using disparate protocols and operating systems, which makes uniform security policy enforcement nearly impossible \cite{Mavroeidis2023}. This complexity is amplified by the large-scale, dynamic nature of these networks and the physical vulnerability of devices often deployed in unsecured locations \cite{AlGaradi2020}.

Consequently, conventional security tools are fundamentally ill-equipped for this environment. Signature-based Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS), the cornerstone of many legacy systems, rely on static databases of known attack patterns. This approach is purely reactive and fails against novel or zero-day attacks --- threats for which no signature yet exists \cite{Khraisat2019,Yang2024}. While anomaly-based IDS attempt to overcome this by baselining ``normal'' behavior, the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of IoT traffic makes establishing a reliable baseline exceptionally difficult, leading to high false positive rates that can disrupt critical functions \cite{Ibitoye2021,Tharewal2022}.

The core limitation of these paradigms is their lack of adaptability. They are built on static models and cannot autonomously evolve. This creates a critical need for a new security paradigm: one that is forward-looking, data-driven, and capable of autonomous decision-making. \textbf{Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)}, a field of machine learning focused on goal-oriented learning from interaction, has emerged as a powerful framework for such a system \cite{Chen2021}. A DRL agent learns an optimal decision-making strategy, or policy, through trial-and-error interaction with its environment. Unlike static systems, a DRL agent can generalize from its experience to respond to novel threats and can adapt its policy as new adversarial tactics emerge, making it well-suited for the non-stationary nature of IoT security \cite{Sutton2018,Uprety2021}. Algorithms such as Deep Q-Networks (DQN) \cite{Mnih2015} and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \cite{Schulman2017} exemplify this paradigm, and are among the methods evaluated in this dissertation. By framing cyber-defense as a sequential decision-making problem, DRL provides a robust mathematical foundation for creating autonomous security systems that can learn to outmaneuver attackers \cite{Gueriani2023}.

\section{Problem Statement and Contributions}\label{sec:proposal_and_contributions}
The preceding analysis culminates in a clear research problem: existing IoT security frameworks are rarely simultaneously \textbf{adaptive}, \textbf{data-driven}, and \textbf{benchmarked} against deployable reference policies. Underlying any such comparison, however, is a more subtle question: \emph{when} does a learned sequential policy genuinely outperform a memoryless supervised classifier, and when is its apparent advantage merely an artifact of how the task was posed? If each network flow individually reveals the attacker's intent, then the defense problem collapses into per-flow classification, and a well-tuned classifier is the natural --- and hard to beat --- solution. Reinforcement learning earns its keep only when the decision is genuinely \emph{sequential} and \emph{partially observed}: when no single observation is sufficient and the defender must accumulate evidence over time before committing to a response.

This dissertation addresses that question directly. We design, implement, and evaluate a closed-loop adaptive defense framework for IoT networks in which a kill-chain-aware reinforcement-learning defender confronts a reactive adversary over real CICIoT2023 feature rows replayed as session-coherent kill-chain trajectories. The central modeling commitment is to make the defense a \emph{genuine} partial-observability problem rather than a disguised classification problem. Feature rows from adjacent kill-chain stages are statistically \textbf{aliased} at a controllable rate, so that no single row identifies the stage and the defender can only infer it across a temporal window. This deliberately removes a simplification present in earlier formulations of the task, namely independent per-step feature sampling, under which a memoryless classifier is optimal by construction and reinforcement learning has no structural reason to win. Concretely, this work makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A partially-observable kill-chain defense environment.} A closed-loop Gymnasium architecture combining (i) a reactive \textbf{tug-of-war attacker} that walks the kill chain and responds to the defender's force --- a proportionate action pushes it one stage back, an under-forced action lets it advance, and an over-forced action merely holds it --- and (ii) a five-action defender (observe, log, restrict, block, isolate) that \textbf{never observes the true kill-chain stage}. Escalation pressure is \textbf{coupled to the attacker's proximity to the impact stage} rather than to any fixed intrusion budget. Observations are drawn \textbf{session-coherently} from real CICIoT2023 rows~\cite{Neto2023}, and adjacent-stage \textbf{observation aliasing} at rate $\alpha$ makes stage inference a true POMDP. The CICIoT2023 dataset is projected onto a five-stage Cyber Kill Chain (benign, recon, access, maneuver, impact) with a documented per-class mapping; ten attack classes are held out disjointly from training and used as feature-injection distribution-shift stress tests.

    \item \textbf{A controlled crossover isolating where windowed RL beats a supervised classifier.} Sweeping the aliasing rate $\alpha$ traces a crossover from parity to a statistically separated PPO advantage. At $\alpha=0$ --- the unaliased, lowest-ambiguity regime, where stage evidence is strongest but still inferred from features rather than handed to the policy --- the windowed PPO agent and the supervised RandomForest acting baseline (RF-Acting) are statistically tied, with PPO scoring marginally \emph{below} RF-Acting (\AnchorPPO{} versus \AnchorRF{}, overlapping confidence intervals): the redesigned task does not favor RL by construction. As $\alpha$ grows, RF-Acting degrades monotonically (from \AnchorRF{} all the way down to \AlphaOneRF{} at full aliasing $\alpha=1.0$, where the memoryless classifier turns net-harmful and scores below zero) while PPO holds essentially flat (\AnchorPPO{} to \AlphaOnePPO{}, the intermediate points varying non-monotonically within overlapping confidence intervals), and from the headline operating point $\alpha=\HeadlineAlpha{}$ onward PPO beats RF-Acting with \emph{disjoint} bootstrap confidence intervals (gap $\AlphaFourGap{}$, widening to $\AlphaOneGap{}$ at $\alpha=1.0$). The advantage is therefore scoped to the imposed partial-observability stress and the reactive attacker model tested here, rather than claimed as a dataset-wide or reward-independent law.

    \item \textbf{A coupled-versus-decoupled reward ablation that addresses the privileged-reward objection.} The objection is that a reward shaped by a stage-action proportionality term hands the agent a privileged classification target, making a supervised classifier sufficient. We test it by training under both a \emph{coupled} (proportionality-shaped) and a \emph{decoupled} (sparse outcome-only) reward. In this partially observable setting, the best RL agent beats RF-Acting under \emph{both} reward regimes (coupled gap $\CouplingGapCoupled{}$, decoupled gap $\CouplingGapOutcome{}$, RF behind in both); the reward shaping only changes \emph{which} algorithm wins, not whether the learned policy separates from RF-Acting. The privileged reward is a learnable shortcut, not a precondition for the observed advantage.

    \item \textbf{A held-out attack-class prevention advantage.} Against ten attack classes reserved from training and injected at their corresponding kill-chain stages, the windowed RL defender prevents a substantially larger fraction of intrusions than RF-Acting on \emph{every} class (best windowed RL agent $\FfifteenBestRLPreventionLow$--$\FfifteenBestRLPreventionHigh$ versus RF $\FfifteenRFPreventionLow$--$\FfifteenRFPreventionHigh$; best-RL-minus-RF advantage $\FfifteenAdvantageLow$ to $\FfifteenAdvantageHigh$), and the advantage shows \textbf{no detectable dependence on the upstream detector's per-class recall} in the direction the disguised-classification objection predicts (Spearman $\rho = \FfifteenSpearmanRho$, $p = \FfifteenSpearmanP$; Pearson $r = \FfifteenPearsonR$, $p = \FfifteenPearsonP$, neither significant; bootstrap slope CI spanning zero, on $n=10$ classes). Prevention demands sustained, well-timed proportional pressure across the episode --- a temporal-control property that no memoryless per-flow classifier can reproduce under the tested protocol, however accurately it labels any individual flow.

    \item \textbf{An honest account of algorithm reliability and a reproducible artifact chain.} Under the sparse outcome-only reward and a fixed 5{,}000{,}000-step budget with no early stopping, the two on-policy algorithms (PPO and Advantage Actor-Critic, A2C) both clear the negative regime and train reliably: PPO's across-seed standard deviation is $\approx 15$ and A2C's is even tighter at $\approx 9$, whereas the off-policy DQN --- once the per-step shaping is removed --- loses its top rank and destabilizes, with several times that variance (across-seed sd $\approx 52$) and a slightly negative mean in the reward-coupling ablation ($\CouplingDQNOutcome$). A2C reaches the highest best-checkpoint outcome reward at the headline $\alpha = \HeadlineAlpha$ ($\AlphaFourATwoC$ versus $\AlphaFourPPO$), and the reward-coupling ablation isolates this as an optimization-stability property of the on-policy family rather than of PPO alone. Algorithm choice also surfaces distinct learned doctrines: A2C strangles the attacker's mid-chain advance (a \emph{prevent-at-maneuver} policy), whereas PPO admits the late stage and isolates afterward (a \emph{contain-at-impact} policy). We retain PPO as the pinned defender for the F10/F17 fixed-policy sweeps because its flatter learning-curve plateau makes the off-distribution sweeps cleaner to read, but A2C carries the highest outcome-reward policy at the headline. These dynamics are reported in full rather than omitted. Every figure ships with a manifest that pins its inputs and the producing commit, and the full pipeline is reproducible end-to-end (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this dissertation is structured as follows. Chapter~\ref{cap:background} reviews background concepts in IoT security, the Cyber Kill Chain, and reinforcement learning, and situates this work against related research. Chapter~\ref{cap:methodology} details the framework architecture, including the kill-chain projection of CICIoT2023, the tug-of-war attacker and stage detector, the partially-observable Markov Decision Process underlying the environment, the reward function, the training protocol, and the baseline policies. Chapter~\ref{cap:results} presents the empirical results --- the aliasing crossover, the coupled-versus-decoupled reward ablation, and the out-of-distribution robustness story --- and discusses their implications. Chapter~\ref{cap:conclusion} summarizes the contributions, discusses limitations and threats to validity, and outlines future work.
