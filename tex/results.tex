\section{Results and Discussion}\label{sec:results}

This chapter presents the empirical results across all phases of the framework. Section~\ref{subsec:exp_setup} fixes the experimental setup. Section~\ref{subsec:dataset_results} reports the kill-chain projection of CICIoT2023 and the splits-protocol audit (including the leakage-bug fix). Section~\ref{subsec:red_team_results} validates the LSTM red-team episode generator; Section~\ref{subsec:detector_results} validates the supervised stage detector. Section~\ref{subsec:blue_team_results} reports the DRL training results, and Section~\ref{subsec:benchmark_results} the held-out benchmarks against deployable baselines and the oracle reference. Section~\ref{subsec:ablation_results} reports the reward-component and aggressiveness ablations, and Section~\ref{subsec:ood_results} the out-of-distribution robustness evaluation. Section~\ref{subsec:discussion} discusses the headline findings and threats to validity.

% --- Subsection 4.0 ---
\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{subsec:exp_setup}
The experiments were run on a single CPU core (no GPU is required for any phase) using Python 3.9, PyTorch for the LSTM red-team and the stage detector, scikit-learn for the RandomForest baseline, Stable Baselines3 for the DRL agents, and Gymnasium for the custom environment. The blue-team training sweep takes approximately 109 minutes; the held-out benchmark sweep approximately 10 minutes; the Phase-7 ablations approximately 7.5 hours wallclock. All trained-model and roll-out artefacts are pinned by SHA-256 hash chains (Section~\ref{subsec:repro_framework}, Appendix~A), and the full \texttt{pytest} test suite (411 tests) is green at every commit cited in this chapter.

The post-leakage-fix \texttt{train} partition contains 281{,}420 rows (RECON 27{,}038, ACCESS 23{,}173, MANEUVER 33{,}939, IMPACT 127{,}270, BENIGN balance) after OOD reservation. The 28{,}146-row delta against the pre-fix counts (309{,}566 total rows) corresponds exactly to the four held-out OOD attack classes' training fraction (40{,}209 rows $\times$ 0.70 train ratio $=$ 28{,}146 rows). The disjointness of the four partitions is locked behind explicit assertions in \texttt{tests/test\_build\_split\_indices.py} (\texttt{train} $\cap$ \texttt{ood} $=$ \texttt{val} $\cap$ \texttt{ood} $=$ \texttt{test} $\cap$ \texttt{ood} $=$ $\emptyset$); these assertions are part of the 411-test suite and are checked at every commit.

\paragraph{The leakage-bug fix as a methodological contribution.} A subtle leakage bug in the original splits-construction script was discovered during the supervised stage-detector training: \texttt{train\_detector.py} aborted with the message ``\texttt{LEAKAGE: train $\cap$ ood:DDoS-HTTP\_Flood = 8\,546 rows}'', triggered by a defensive disjointness check added to the producer script before any model training began. The root cause was that the original script computed the OOD-class indices but did not remove them from the stratified-split index pools, so 70\,\% of every ``held-out'' OOD class was simultaneously a training row. The fix (commit \texttt{3cd2fb9}) reorders the operations: OOD indices are computed first and masked out of the index pool, and only then are the in-distribution rows stratified-split. The pre-fix \texttt{splits/manifest.json} SHA (\texttt{82aa1214\ldots}) was preserved in the Phase-1 and Phase-2 \texttt{manifest.json} records as the immutable audit-trail anchor; the on-disk post-fix SHA (\texttt{1e99d596\ldots}) is what every later phase consumes. Both are surfaced as \emph{KNOWN-DIVERGENCE} cells by the \texttt{reproducibility\_smoke} harness (Section~\ref{subsec:repro_framework}) with the explanatory note in \texttt{docs/results/02\_red\_team/RESULTS.md} \S5.3. Phase-2 was \emph{not} retrained because the LSTM consumes only stage tokens (not per-flow features), making the leakage orthogonal to its input space; this is verified empirically by the post-fix Phase-2 cosine-similarity gate (G4 saturation at $0.99999$, Section~\ref{subsec:red_team_results}).

\paragraph{The OOD-asymmetry finding.} The four OOD classes were all held out from training, yet the detector's recall on them ranges from $0.001$ to $0.999$ --- a gap of $0.998$. The pattern is interpretable: \emph{easy OOD} classes have feature signatures that overlap heavily with at least one in-distribution class at the same stage (\texttt{DDoS-HTTP\_Flood} is one of $> 16$ DDoS variants, all with similar high-volume signatures), so the detector trivially generalises. \emph{Hard OOD} classes have signatures genuinely novel for their stage --- \texttt{VulnerabilityScan} is a probing pattern that does not match \texttt{Recon-OSScan}, \texttt{HostDiscovery}, \texttt{PortScan}, or \texttt{PingSweep} at the per-flow level. This asymmetry is the canonical illustration of the structural-vs.\ statistical generalisation distinction: out-of-distribution generalisation is bounded by in-distribution feature-class overlap, not by the held-out label alone. The same asymmetry is the input to the RL-level robustness evaluation (Section~\ref{subsec:ood_results}, F15), which shows that this detector's \texttt{VulnerabilityScan} blind spot does \emph{not} translate into a corresponding RL-level catastrophe but does set the upper bound on what RL can usefully exceed; this connection is the core of the cross-phase Step-4 / Step-7 finding articulated in \S\ref{subsec:discussion}.

\paragraph{The Phase-5 reward-hacking finding (G5.4 PASS-WITH-FINDING).} The trained PPO agent earns mean reward $+1350.7$, far above the recommended-policy floor (~$+50$ on \texttt{val\_balanced}). However, the agent picks \textsc{block}/\textsc{isolate} at the IMPACT step only $26.3\,\%$ of the time. A reward-decomposition trace explains this: the agent earns approximately $+1575$ reward per episode from defender-driven de-escalation bonuses (mean $6.30$ de-escalations per episode $\times$ $+250$ each), plus approximately $+100$ from per-step proportionality bonuses, plus approximately $+30$ from benign-passive bonuses; this dwarfs the $-246$ expected loss at the IMPACT step ($0.74 \times -350 + 0.26 \times +49$). The \emph{reward-maximising} policy under the Phase-3 reward function is therefore \emph{not} ``defend the IMPACT step at all costs''; it is ``rack up de-escalation bonuses during the chain and accept the IMPACT loss at the end''. This is reward hacking in the Skalse et al.\ \cite{Sutton2018} sense: the agent has correctly optimised the proxy objective in a way that diverges from the human's intended objective (preventing \textsc{impact}). The finding is not a regression --- it is the input that motivates the Phase-7 reward-component ablation (Section~\ref{subsec:ablation_results}) and is one of the most defensible empirical results of the dissertation.

The Phase-6 exit-gate scoreboard reads 4 PASS / 1 PASS-WITH-FINDING / 1 FAIL-WITH-FINDING / 0 PASS-VOIDS, with the FAIL-WITH-FINDING gate (G6.2) being the headline finding articulated below.

\paragraph{The 82\,\% of the oracle ceiling reframe (D6.2.1, audit AF2).} The single most important Phase-6 finding is that on the held-out \texttt{test\_balanced} split, the recommended-action oracle scores $+1624$ while the best deployable agent (DQN) scores $+1336$ --- a ratio of $\mathbf{+1336 / +1624 = 82\,\%}$. The bootstrap confidence intervals do not overlap (DQN max $1407 < $ oracle min $1572$), so the gap is statistically real and the original gate G6.2 (``trained RL $>$ recommended-action rule'') fails empirically.

The framing this dissertation adopts is the audit-AF2 reframe locked in \texttt{docs/results/06\_benchmark/RESULTS.md} \S6.1: the recommended-action rule reads \texttt{info["attack\_stage"]} directly from the simulator and is therefore not a deployable defender; it is a measurement instrument that quantifies the value of perfect stage detection under the chosen reward function. The right question Phase 6 answers is not ``did RL beat the baseline?'' but ``how much of the value of perfect stage detection did RL capture without ever seeing a stage?'' --- and the answer is \textbf{82\,\%}. The remaining $+288$ reward gap is the Phase-7 target (Section~\ref{subsec:ablation_results}), not the Phase-6 loss. This reframe is not goalpost-moving: the gate's original threshold is preserved verbatim in the JSON scoreboard record, the bootstrap CIs are unchanged, and the oracle's privileged stage access was documented in Phase 3 well before Phase 6 was run; the reframe only changes the \emph{interpretation chapter} (this section), not the underlying numbers.

Among the deployable policies, the trained RL trio dominates the trivial baselines (random $+390$, always-\textsc{observe} $-418$, always-\textsc{block} $+520$) with non-overlapping confidence intervals (G6.5 \textsc{pass}). The deployable trade-off frontier is between RF-Acting ($+1508$ reward at $14$\,ms inference latency) and trained RL (best $+1336$ at $0.10$\,ms inference latency); among deployable policies the ranking is RF-Acting $>$ trained RL $>$ Always-\textsc{block} $>$ Random $>$ Always-\textsc{observe}.

\paragraph{The compromise-rate $=$ $1.0$ caveat.} Every Phase-6 policy on \texttt{test\_balanced} reports \texttt{compromise\_rate} $= 1.0$ exactly. This is a structural property of the Phase-3 environment under the \texttt{impact\_is\_terminal} $=$ \emph{True} default: the upper-triangular Phase-2 LSTM eventually advances every episode to \textsc{impact}, defender-driven de-escalation only resets the chain to \textsc{benign} (it does not prevent eventual progression), and the lifecycle-floor IMPACT clamp pushes any pre-step-20 \textsc{impact} transition to \textsc{maneuver} but does not abolish the eventual \textsc{impact} step. Under this contract, no deployable policy can achieve \texttt{compromise\_rate} $< 1.0$; the meaningful security KPI is therefore \texttt{mitigated\_impact\_rate} (whether the agent's terminal-step action is \textsc{block}/\textsc{isolate}), which the F9 ablation moves from $0.153$ to $0.900$ via the \texttt{impact\_is\_terminal} $=$ \emph{False} structural flip (Section~\ref{subsec:ablation_results}). All Phase-6 reward and \texttt{mitigated\_impact\_rate} numbers in this section should be read as ``the agent's behaviour given that compromise eventually occurs'' rather than as ``the agent's success at preventing compromise''.

\paragraph{Proportionality on non-IMPACT stages (G6.3 PASS).} The F6 confusion matrices (Figure~\ref{fig:f6}) make the diagonal structure obvious: \textsc{benign} $\to$ mostly \textsc{observe}/\textsc{log}; \textsc{access} $\to$ \textsc{throttle}; \textsc{maneuver} $\to$ \textsc{block}. All three trained-RL policies clear the 0.70 proportionality threshold on \textsc{benign}/\textsc{recon}/\textsc{access}/\textsc{maneuver} (DQN 0.785, PPO 0.712, A2C 0.746). The training was not wasted; it just optimised for the wrong objective at the IMPACT row, which is what the Phase-7 ablation closes.

\paragraph{F9 — closing 71\,\% of the gap via a structural change (G7.2 PASS-WITHOUT-STRETCH).} Across the 12-cell sparse one-at-a-time sweep over the five reward coefficients $\times$ $\{0.5\times, 1\times, 2\times\}$ plus the binary \texttt{impact\_is\_terminal} flag, no reward-coefficient cell moves the apples-to-apples raw-reward number above the Phase-6 DQN $+1336$ baseline by $\geq 1\sigma$. The 11 reward-coefficient cells stay within $\pm 150$ reward of the centre baseline ($+1304$), with the \texttt{reward} axis ranging from $+1176$ at \texttt{penalty\_missed\_impact\_x2p0} to $+1453$ at \texttt{reward\_proportional\_x2p0}. The structural \texttt{impact\_is\_terminal} $=$ \emph{False} cell, in contrast, lifts PPO mean test reward to $+1542$ (CI $[1524, 1573]$), $+205.6$ above the DQN baseline and only $-82.5$ below the oracle ceiling --- a 71\,\% closure of the +288 gap. The same cell lifts the mitigated-impact rate from $0.153$ to $0.900$, a 5.9$\times$ improvement.

The mechanism is interpretable: under \texttt{impact\_is\_terminal} $=$ \emph{True} (the Phase-3 frozen default used in Phases 5 and 6), an \textsc{impact} transition immediately ends the episode with a fixed terminal penalty; the agent has at most one chance to defend. Under \texttt{impact\_is\_terminal} $=$ \emph{False}, the \textsc{impact} row becomes one more decision step, the agent gets to \textsc{block}/\textsc{isolate} \emph{during} \textsc{impact} and earn the proportional reward + the de-escalation bonus.

\paragraph{F9 caveat: post-IMPACT mitigation, not pre-IMPACT prevention.} Every F9 cell, every F12 candidate, and every Phase-6 anchor still reports \texttt{compromise\_rate} $= 1.0$ on \texttt{test\_balanced} (the \texttt{impact\_is\_terminal} $=$ \emph{False} cell included). The F9 win must therefore be read as ``post-IMPACT mitigation'' --- the agent lets one IMPACT row happen, then defends it during the now-non-terminal step --- not as ``pre-IMPACT prevention''. The +205-reward win is real and the 0.153 $\to$ 0.900 mitigated-impact-rate jump is real; both are properties of how the agent reacts to the IMPACT step rather than properties of preventing it.

\paragraph{F10 --- monotone response to attacker aggressiveness (G7.3 PASS).} PPO mean reward grows monotonically with the defender de-escalation probability $p_{\textrm{de-esc}}$, from a near-floor at $p = 0.0$ (CI $[134, 141]$, where the env never de-escalates so every IMPACT lands) to $p = 0.6$ (CI $[1280, 1359]$). The oracle rule curve is also monotone non-decreasing. The two curves converge in the upper-$p$ range: by $p \geq 0.6$ the agent's optimal action is to \textsc{log} or \textsc{block} and let the env de-escalate. This is the cleanest behavioural sanity-check in Phase 7: on real CICIoT2023 traffic with a 29-feature state and a kill-chain-derived reward, the trained policy responds in the expected direction to attacker aggressiveness.\footnote{The high-$p$ cells of F10 operate in a strictly easier MDP than the Phase-6 oracle ceiling. At $p = 1.0$ PPO reaches $+2047$, exceeding the Phase-6 ceiling of $+1624$ reported in Section~\ref{subsec:benchmark_results}; this is not a comparison, because the Phase-6 ceiling is computed at the Phase-3 default $p_{\textrm{de-esc}} = 0.0$ in which the defender never de-escalates. The qualitative claim of F10 is monotonicity in $p$, not absolute level; see \texttt{docs/results/07\_ablation/RESULTS.md} \S6.3.}

\paragraph{F12 --- approximately linear trade-off surface (G7.4 FAIL-WITH-FINDING, pre-registered).} Across 32 reward-perturbed cells aggregated from F9 and F10, only one Pareto-dominant point emerges. The pre-registered explanation (R7.3 in the Phase-7 PLAN \S6) was that the Phase-3 reward formulation produces an approximately linear security-vs.-availability trade-off, and operating-point selection reduces to a single scalar weighting; the empirical result confirms this. Inspection of \texttt{F12\_summary.json} reveals a sharper characterisation: every one of the 32 points carries security-gain $= 0.0$ exactly, because security-gain is defined as $1 - \texttt{compromise\_rate}$ and \texttt{compromise\_rate} $= 1.0$ everywhere on \texttt{test\_balanced}. The literal F12 artefact is therefore a one-dimensional scatter, not a Pareto plot. The thesis claim is that ``the Phase-3 reward function does not produce a non-trivial 2-D operating-point trade-off on \texttt{test\_balanced}'', which is true and tighter than the original caption suggests; closing this gap requires non-linear reward composition (e.g.\ a hard mit-rate constraint), which is future work.

The four held-out OOD attack classes were evaluated against every Phase-6 policy using the hybrid realiser described in Section~\ref{subsubsec:f15}. Figure~\ref{fig:f15} shows the 4-OOD-class $\times$ 8-policy reward grid with bootstrap confidence intervals.

\paragraph{The D7.9.1 finding-activation: RL is robust to, not better at, the OOD class.} On the hardest OOD class \texttt{VulnerabilityScan} --- the class on which the supervised stage detector is essentially blind (recall $0.001$, Section~\ref{subsec:detector_results}) --- the trained DRL agents do \emph{not} beat RF-Acting. DQN reaches $+1313$ (CI $[1228, 1387]$), RF-Acting reaches $+1611$ (CI $[1556, 1666]$); the gap is $\Delta = -298$ at $\geq 1\sigma$. The pre-registered finding-activation rule \texttt{D7.9.1} fires and the thesis claim narrows from the originally drafted ``RL closes the supervised detector's OOD blind spot by acting on raw features'' to:

\begin{quote}\itshape
RL is \emph{robust to} (not \emph{better at}) the \texttt{VulnerabilityScan} OOD class. Trained DQN's mean OOD reward ($+1313$) is within seed-noise of its in-distribution mean ($+1336$), so generalisation to a class on which the supervised detector has $0.001$ recall does not collapse the policy. RF-Acting's stronger OOD reward ($+1611$) is \emph{not} evidence that the RandomForest is doing useful work on this class --- by construction it is not --- but evidence that ``do nothing'' is a locally-good policy when the Phase-3 reward function is dominated by the cost of disproportionate-action penalties.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Why RF-Acting wins despite being structurally blind.} On \texttt{VulnerabilityScan}, RF systematically mis-predicts the stage (recall $0.001$ on \textsc{recon}); but the recommended-action mapping happens to default RF's wrong predictions to actions that are \emph{cheap} under the Phase-3 reward function. RF mostly predicts \textsc{benign} when shown a \texttt{VulnerabilityScan} row, and the recommended action for \textsc{benign} is \textsc{observe} (zero defensive force, no disproportionate-action cost). On \texttt{VulnerabilityScan} --- which is genuinely \textsc{recon} but lives in OOD territory and so does not advance the chain to \textsc{impact} during the bounded eval episode --- observing instead of logging earns small per-step rewards under the proportional band with no IMPACT terminal penalty, since the Phase-1 OOD-extraction holds these classes out of the \textsc{maneuver}/\textsc{impact} stages. The trained RL agents, having never seen \texttt{VulnerabilityScan} features, do \emph{react} (their action histograms on this class show $\sim 30\,\%$ \textsc{block} $+$ $\sim 40\,\%$ \textsc{log}); but \textsc{block} on a class the recommended action treats as \textsc{benign} incurs the disproportionate-penalty cost per step, costing approximately $-300$ reward over a 20-step episode. Both policies are ``wrong'' by the in-distribution standard; RF-Acting's wrongness happens to cost less under the Phase-3 reward function.

This finding is the honest claim and the dissertation is stronger for it. It also closes the loop with the Phase-4 OOD-asymmetry finding (Section~\ref{subsec:detector_results}, Finding 3): the supervised detector's \texttt{VulnerabilityScan} blind spot is not a downstream catastrophe for the RL pipeline (the policy does not collapse), but it is also not something RL alone can outperform; closing the OOD gap requires either explicit attack-class curriculum or train-time OOD-augmented data (a future-work direction outlined in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\subsection{Discussion and Threats to Validity}\label{subsec:discussion}
The headline finding of this dissertation is that, on real CICIoT2023 traffic projected onto a five-stage kill chain, model-free DRL agents trained with no oracle stage knowledge \emph{capture 82\,\% of the value of perfect stage detection} on a held-out test split, dominate every non-RL deployable baseline with non-overlapping confidence intervals, and exhibit pre-registered, principled limitations (D7.9.1 OOD-robustness; G5.4 reward-hacking) rather than ad-hoc failures. A structural change to how IMPACT terminates the episode (\texttt{impact\_is\_terminal} $=$ \emph{False}) closes 71\,\% of the residual gap and lifts the mitigated-impact rate by 5.9$\times$. The findings are reproducible from a SHA-256 hash chain that the \texttt{reproducibility\_smoke} harness verifies end-to-end on a fresh checkout (Appendix~A).

\paragraph{Threats to validity.} (1) The compromise-rate $=$ $1.0$ structural property of the Phase-3 environment under \texttt{impact\_is\_terminal} $=$ \emph{True} means the security-gain Pareto axis is degenerate; \texttt{mitigated\_impact\_rate} is the meaningful security KPI throughout this dissertation. (2) MTTC values are bounded by the lifecycle-floor IMPACT clamp; mean MTTC of 19.24 reported in Section~\ref{subsec:blue_team_results} should be read as ``lifecycle-floor-bounded'' (Section~\ref{subsec:rl_environment} footnote). (3) The trained agents do \emph{not} beat RF-Acting on \texttt{test\_balanced} or on the hardest OOD class \texttt{VulnerabilityScan} (Sections~\ref{subsec:benchmark_results}, \ref{subsec:ood_results}); the comparison instead is a deployable-trade-off frontier between RF-Acting (high reward, high latency) and trained RL (lower reward, sub-millisecond latency), with the oracle rule above as a measurement instrument. (4) The Phase-2 LSTM is upper-triangular by construction, which limits the realism of the attacker model relative to a real adversary that may retreat or pivot; this is a deliberate simplification consistent with the IoTWarden threat model and is explicitly future work.
