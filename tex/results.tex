\chapter{Results and Discussion}\label{cap:results}

This chapter presents the empirical results across all stages of the framework. Section~\ref{sec:exp_setup} fixes the experimental setup. Section~\ref{sec:dataset_results} reports the kill-chain projection of CICIoT2023 and the splits protocol, including the data-leakage fix. Section~\ref{sec:blue_team_results} reports the blue-team training results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the headline findings and threats to validity.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:exp_setup}
The experiments were run on a single CPU core (no GPU is required for any development stage) using Python, PyTorch for the stage detector, scikit-learn for the RandomForest baseline, Stable~Baselines3 for the reinforcement-learning agents, and Gymnasium for the custom environment. The reactive tug-of-war attacker is specified in closed form and requires no training. Each learned agent is trained to an evaluation-reward plateau under a generous step cap with early stopping (Section~\ref{sec:drl_training}). The best checkpoint, not the last, is carried to evaluation. Every figure in this chapter is regenerated from a manifest that pins its input artefacts and producing script (Section~\ref{sec:repro_framework}, Appendix~\ref{app:repro}), and the full \texttt{pytest} suite (\NumTests{} tests) passes on a clean checkout.

\paragraph{The data-leakage fix as a methodological contribution.} A subtle leakage bug in the original splits-construction script was discovered during supervised stage-detector training: a disjointness assertion in the split-generation pipeline aborted before any model training began, reporting that a held-out class was simultaneously present in the training pool. The root cause was that the original script computed the held-out-class indices but did not remove them from the stratified-split index pools, so the majority of every ``held-out'' class was also a training row. The fix reorders the operations: the held-out indices are computed first and masked out of the pool, and only then are the in-distribution rows stratified and split. This bug would have inflated every downstream out-of-distribution number. Surfacing it through an explicit verification check, rather than discovering it after publication, exemplifies the defensive practice this dissertation argues for. The attacker model is unaffected by the fix, because it consumes only kill-chain stage tokens rather than per-flow features.

\paragraph{A graded recall spectrum, not a clean split.} The ten held-out classes were all reserved from training, yet the tuned RandomForest's recall on them ranges from $0.20$ to $0.998$. Out-of-distribution recall is bounded by how much a class's feature signature overlaps an in-distribution class \emph{at its own stage}: \texttt{VulnerabilityScan} probes in a way unlike any trained \textsc{recon} scanner, whereas \texttt{DDoS-HTTP\_Flood} is one of many high-volume \textsc{impact}-stage floods. This spectrum is the independent axis of the out-of-distribution robustness evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:ood_results}: it lets us ask not merely whether the learned policy survives a blind spot, but whether its advantage tracks the detector's recall or is independent of it.

The three feedforward reinforcement-learning algorithms (PPO, DQN, A2C) were trained over \NumSeeds{} seeds each, under the primary outcome-reward contract and at the headline observation-aliasing rate $\alpha = \HeadlineAlpha$. Figure~\ref{fig:f3} shows the episodic-reward learning curves; Figure~\ref{fig:f4} shows the per-stage action distribution of the trained PPO policy.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figs/F4_action_distribution.pdf}
    \caption{Per-stage action distribution of the trained PPO policy under the sparse outcome reward. The policy applies a graded force ladder --- light observation/logging at benign and reconnaissance stages, escalating to restriction, blocking, and isolation as the inferred threat advances --- learned purely from accumulated feature evidence rather than from any per-step stage label.}
    \label{fig:f4}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Only on-policy PPO is stable under the sparse outcome reward.} Under the primary outcome-reward contract the agent receives no per-step shaping toward the stage-recommended action: reward arrives only at episode termination, as a function of whether the attacker was prevented or compromised. This is a deliberately hard learning signal, and the three algorithms respond very differently to it. PPO, an on-policy method that aggregates long rollouts before each update, converges to a stable policy across all \NumSeeds{} seeds. DQN and A2C do not: their evaluation reward oscillates throughout training and never settles, reflecting the difficulty of credit assignment over a long horizon with a sparse, delayed terminal signal. Tuning their hyperparameters --- a larger replay buffer and slower target updates for DQN, longer rollouts for A2C (Appendix~\ref{app:hparams}) --- did not stabilise them. We report this rather than omit it: \textbf{only the long-rollout on-policy method learns a stable policy under the sparse outcome reward}, and PPO is therefore the headline reinforcement-learning agent throughout, with DQN and A2C retained as comparison points that expose this sensitivity. This is itself a finding about algorithm choice for sequential security control, not a tuning artefact.

\paragraph{The reward-coupling contract.} The naive alternative is a \emph{coupled} reward that pays a per-step bonus for choosing exactly the action recommended for the current stage --- precisely the quantity a supervised stage classifier predicts. Reporting only the coupled contract would conflate ``the agent learned to defend'' with ``the agent learned to imitate a stage-to-action lookup table'', and would make a supervised classifier the natural optimum by construction. We therefore train and benchmark under the sparse outcome reward throughout, and treat the coupled contract only as a controlled ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}) whose purpose is to show that the learned advantage does not depend on that shaping.

\section{Held-Out Benchmark: Windowed RL versus a Per-Flow Classifier under Partial Observability}\label{sec:benchmark_results}
The central question of this dissertation is \emph{when} a learned sequential policy beats a memoryless per-flow classifier. The held-out benchmark answers it by sweeping the observation-aliasing rate $\alpha$ --- the probability that a step emits a feature row drawn from a stage adjacent to the true one, so that no single row identifies the stage. The same aliased observation stream is fed identically to every policy, so none is privileged. Each trained checkpoint and baseline is evaluated on \texttt{test\_balanced} ($n = 300$ deterministic episodes per policy) at four aliasing rates, $\alpha \in \{0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6\}$. Figure~\ref{fig:falpha} shows the resulting reward curves and Table~\ref{tab:alpha_curve} the underlying numbers.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering

\paragraph{The crossover is the central result.} At $\alpha = 0$ --- the fully observable regime in which a single row identifies the stage --- the per-flow RandomForest is at its strongest and the two policies are statistically tied: PPO scores $\AlphaZeroPPO$ against RF-Acting's $\AlphaZeroRF$, with overlapping confidence intervals (PPO-minus-RF gap $\AlphaZeroGap$). This tie is the crucial anchor: \textbf{the redesigned environment does not favour reinforcement learning by construction}; where the task really is per-flow classification, the classifier wins or ties. As the aliasing rate rises, the picture diverges sharply. RF-Acting, which must commit to an action from a single ambiguous row, degrades monotonically: $\AlphaTwoRF$ at $\alpha = 0.2$, $\AlphaFourRF$ at $\alpha = 0.4$, and $\AlphaSixRF$ at $\alpha = 0.6$. Windowed PPO, which integrates evidence across the recent observation window, holds roughly flat: $\AlphaTwoPPO$, $\AlphaFourPPO$, $\AlphaSixPPO$. The PPO-minus-RF gap widens from $\AlphaTwoGap$ at $\alpha = 0.2$ (still \AlphaTwoVerdict{}) to $\AlphaFourGap$ at $\alpha = 0.4$ and $\AlphaSixGap$ at $\alpha = 0.6$, where the confidence intervals are disjoint (\AlphaFourVerdict{}). The learned agent's advantage therefore appears exactly where theory predicts: \emph{only} once the task becomes a genuine sequential-inference problem rather than a disguised per-flow classification.

\paragraph{The oracle ceiling and the trivial baselines.} The full-observability oracle, which reads the true decision-time stage and applies the recommended action, scores near $\OracleCeiling$ across all aliasing rates and is reported only as a reference ceiling: it bounds the value of perfect stage perception and is not a deployable competitor. The trivial baselines are dominated throughout: always-\textsc{observe} scores about $-350$ (it never acts, so every attack reaches impact), always-\textsc{block} scores near $0$ (it prevents compromise but pays a constant availability cost), and the random policy scores about $-90$. The contrast between the always-\textsc{block} and always-\textsc{observe} extremes confirms that the reward correctly rewards proportionate force rather than either passivity or indiscriminate blocking.

\paragraph{The reward-coupling ablation neutralises the privileged-reward objection.} A natural objection to any reinforcement-learning result on this task is that the reward secretly hands the agent the stage label: if the agent is paid per step for choosing the stage-recommended action, then the optimal policy is simply a stage classifier, and a supervised classifier should match or beat it. The coupled contract reinstates exactly that per-step shaping; the outcome contract removes it. The ablation retrains every learned agent under both contracts and compares the best learned agent against RF-Acting scored under the same contract. The result is decisive: \textbf{the best learned agent beats RF-Acting under both reward contracts}. Under the coupled reward the RF-minus-best-RL gap is $\CouplingGapCoupled$ (best learned agent \CouplingBestCoupled{}, $\CouplingDQNCoupled$, versus RF-Acting), and under the sparse outcome reward it is $\CouplingGapOutcome$ (best learned agent \CouplingBestOutcome{}, versus RF-Acting). The privileged per-step shaping therefore does not explain the learned advantage; once the environment is genuinely partially observable, the supervised classifier loses regardless of the reward. What the reward shaping \emph{does} change is which algorithm wins: the dense coupled signal lets the off-policy DQN exploit a stage-to-action shortcut and score highest ($\CouplingDQNCoupled$), whereas under the sparse outcome reward that shortcut is gone and only the on-policy PPO remains stable (DQN, by contrast, collapses to $\CouplingDQNOutcome$). This is consistent with the training-stability finding of Section~\ref{sec:blue_team_results}.

\section{Out-of-Distribution Robustness}\label{sec:ood_results}
The ten held-out zero-day classes (Section~\ref{ssec:splits}) were evaluated against every benchmark policy using the single-stage feature-injection protocol of Section~\ref{ssec:f15}, at the headline aliasing rate $\alpha = \HeadlineAlpha$. The security metric is the \emph{prevention rate} --- the fraction of episodes in which the attacker is held below the impact stage for the full horizon --- reported in preference to raw reward, which is dominated by accumulated penalties over long episodes. Figure~\ref{fig:f15} shows the per-class, per-policy grid and Figure~\ref{fig:f15b} relates the learned agent's advantage to the detector's recall.

Across every class the windowed PPO policy prevents a substantially larger fraction of attacks than the detector-coupled RF-Acting baseline.}
    \label{fig:f15}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figs/F15b_recall_vs_advantage.pdf}
    \caption{Prevention advantage of windowed PPO over RF-Acting on each held-out class, plotted against the RandomForest's recall on that class. The advantage is uniformly large and positive ($+0.36$ to $+0.55$) and shows no dependence on detector recall: the learned policy's robustness is a property of sequential control under partial observability, not of detector accuracy.}
    \label{fig:f15b}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{A uniform, recall-independent prevention advantage.} On all ten held-out classes, windowed PPO prevents between $0.37$ and $0.57$ of attacks, whereas the detector-coupled RF-Acting baseline prevents between $0.00$ and $0.15$. The PPO-minus-RF prevention advantage is large and positive on every class, ranging from $+0.36$ to $+0.55$, and --- this is the key observation --- it is essentially \emph{flat across the detector's recall spectrum} (Figure~\ref{fig:f15b}). The advantage on \texttt{DoS-SYN\_Flood}, where the RandomForest's recall is $0.998$ ($+0.49$), is comparable to that on \texttt{VulnerabilityScan}, where its recall is just $0.224$ ($+0.40$). Were the learned agent's robustness merely a matter of surviving a blind spot, the advantage would shrink to zero on the near-perfectly-detected classes; it does not.

\paragraph{Why RF-Acting fails to prevent, even where it sees well.} The explanation is structural rather than perceptual. Under the proximity-coupled dynamics, \emph{prevention} requires sustained, well-timed proportional force that holds the attacker below the impact stage for the entire horizon. A memoryless per-flow classifier commits to one action per row and, even when its stage prediction is correct, plays a largely passive action mix: across the held-out classes RF-Acting chooses observation or logging on roughly two-thirds of steps, which lets the attacker advance to impact. It then blocks on the impact step itself and so registers ``mitigated'' rather than ``prevented''. The same pattern holds in-distribution, where RF-Acting prevents only a small fraction of episodes while PPO prevents a majority. Prevention is therefore a temporal-control property that a per-flow classifier cannot express, regardless of how accurately it labels any single flow. This is the sharpest form of the central thesis: the deployment value of acting directly on raw feature sequences is not per-flow labelling accuracy but the ability to exert sustained, proportionate control that a one-shot classifier structurally cannot.

\begin{quote}\itshape
On every one of ten held-out zero-day classes, a learned policy that never consults a stage classifier prevents a substantially larger share of attacks than the strongest supervised baseline, and its advantage does not depend on whether the classifier sees the class well or poorly. Prevention under a reactive adversary is a property of sequential control, not of per-flow detection accuracy.
\end{quote}

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}\label{sec:discussion}
This dissertation set out to answer a sharp methodological objection: that a reinforcement-learning agent on this task is solving a disguised classification problem, so a supervised classifier should match or beat it and the learned policy adds nothing. The three results of this chapter answer that objection directly. First, the held-out benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark_results}) shows that as observation aliasing rises --- making single-row stage inference genuinely ambiguous --- the per-flow RandomForest degrades monotonically while windowed PPO holds flat, with a tie at $\alpha = 0$ (proving the environment does not favour reinforcement learning by construction) and disjoint confidence intervals from $\alpha = \HeadlineAlpha$ onward. Second, the reward-coupling ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}) shows the learned advantage survives \emph{removing} the per-step shaping that would make the task a classification problem: the best learned agent beats RF-Acting under both the coupled and the sparse outcome reward (gaps $\CouplingGapCoupled$ and $\CouplingGapOutcome$). Third, the out-of-distribution evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:ood_results}) shows the learned policy prevents a uniformly larger fraction of ten held-out zero-day attacks than the supervised baseline, with an advantage independent of detector recall, because prevention is a temporal-control property a per-flow classifier cannot express. Together these establish that the learned policy's value is real and specifically a consequence of sequential decision-making under partial observability, not of any privileged signal. A secondary finding is that only the long-rollout on-policy method (PPO) learns stably under the sparse outcome reward; off-policy DQN and short-rollout A2C are unstable regardless of tuning, which we report rather than hide.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}\label{sec:threats_to_validity} (1) \textbf{Designed attacker dynamics.} The attacker is a designed, reactive tug-of-war process whose rule set and proximity-coupled escalation are chosen by the experimenter, not learned or adversarially co-trained; the results therefore hold against this reactive-but-not-co-adapting adversary, and a jointly trained attacker is future work. The evasion-before-commit sweep (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}) shows the learned defense degrades gracefully against a partially reactive attacker, which is the closest relaxation of this assumption tested here. (2) \textbf{Session coherence is a modelling abstraction.} Because the pre-aggregated CICIoT2023 flow records carry no recoverable session key, the session-coherent sampling and the observation aliasing are constructed at the environment layer rather than recovered from raw packet traces. The aliasing rate $\alpha$ is therefore a controlled difficulty knob, and the contribution is the \emph{shape} of the response to it --- the monotone RF degradation against flat PPO, anchored by the $\alpha = 0$ tie --- rather than any single operating point. (3) \textbf{Statistical power.} The learned agents are evaluated over \NumSeeds{} seeds with bootstrap confidence intervals; the headline crossover claims rest on disjoint intervals at $\alpha = 0.4$ and $\alpha = 0.6$, but finer distinctions between the learned algorithms are below the resolution of ten seeds and are not claimed.
