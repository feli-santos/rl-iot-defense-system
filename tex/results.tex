\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:exp_setup}
The experiments were run on a single CPU core (no GPU is required for any development stage) using Python 3.9, PyTorch for the stage detector, scikit-learn for the RandomForest baseline, Stable Baselines3 for the DRL agents, and Gymnasium for the custom environment. The 5$\times$5 Markov attacker is a closed-form transition matrix and requires no training. The blue-team training sweep (30 runs, 10 seeds $\times$ 3 algorithms) takes approximately 3.1 hours; the held-out benchmark sweep approximately 10 minutes; the ablation and OOD-robustness sweeps approximately 7.5 hours wallclock. All trained-model and roll-out artefacts are pinned by SHA-256 hash chains (Section~\ref{sec:repro_framework}, Appendix~A), and the full \texttt{pytest} test suite (\NumTests{} tests) is green at every commit cited in this chapter.

\paragraph{The leakage-bug fix as a methodological contribution.} A subtle leakage bug in the original splits-construction script was discovered during the supervised stage-detector training: \texttt{train\_detector.py} aborted with the message ``\texttt{LEAKAGE: train}~$\cap$~\texttt{ood:DDoS-HTTP\_Flood = 8{,}546 rows}'', triggered by a defensive disjointness check added to the producer script before any model training began. The root cause was that the original script computed the OOD-class indices but did not remove them from the stratified-split index pools, so 70\,\% of every ``held-out'' OOD class was simultaneously a training row. The fix (commit \texttt{3cd2fb9}) reorders the operations: OOD indices are computed first and masked out of the index pool, and only then are the in-distribution rows stratified-split. The pre-fix \texttt{splits/manifest.json} SHA (\texttt{82aa1214\ldots}) was preserved in the dataset-preparation stage \texttt{manifest.json} records as the immutable audit-trail anchor; the on-disk post-fix SHA is what every later stage consumes. The divergence is surfaced as a \emph{KNOWN-DIVERGENCE} cell by the \texttt{reproducibility\_smoke} harness (Section~\ref{sec:repro_framework}). The attacker model is unaffected by the leakage fix: the 5$\times$5 Markov attacker consumes only kill-chain stage tokens (not per-flow features), so the splits divergence is orthogonal to its dynamics.

\paragraph{The OOD-asymmetry finding.} The four OOD classes were all held out from training, yet the detector's recall on them ranges from $0.001$ to $0.999$ --- a gap of $0.999$ --- and splits cleanly into two pairs. The two \emph{easy OOD} classes have feature signatures that overlap heavily with at least one in-distribution class \emph{at their own stage} (\texttt{DDoS-HTTP\_Flood} is one of $> 16$ \textsc{impact}-stage DDoS variants with similar high-volume signatures, recall $0.999$; \texttt{XSS} shares ACCESS-stage HTTP semantics with in-distribution web classes, recall $0.953$), so the detector generalises to them almost for free. The two \emph{hard OOD} classes have signatures genuinely novel for their stage and are effectively blind spots: \texttt{VulnerabilityScan} (\textsc{recon}, recall $0.001$) is a probing pattern that matches none of \texttt{Recon-OSScan}, \texttt{HostDiscovery}, \texttt{PortScan}, or \texttt{PingSweep} at the per-flow level, and \texttt{Mirai-udpplain} (\textsc{maneuver}, recall $0.084$) is systematically \emph{mis-staged}: $11{,}096$ of its $12{,}121$ flows are predicted \textsc{impact}, because its high-volume UDP-flood signature resembles the \textsc{impact}-stage flood classes far more than the \textsc{maneuver}-stage Mirai-\texttt{greeth}/\texttt{greip} variants seen in training. This asymmetry is the canonical illustration of the structural-vs.\ statistical generalisation distinction: out-of-distribution generalisation is bounded by in-distribution feature-class overlap \emph{at the correct stage}, not by the held-out label alone --- and a class whose novel signature collides with a \emph{different} stage's in-distribution signature (Mirai-udpplain) is mis-staged rather than merely missed. The same asymmetry is the input to the RL-level robustness evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:ood_results}, F15).

\paragraph{Reward Mis-specification: Discovered and Structurally Fixed (G5.4 PASS-WITH-FINDING).}
Without a finite intrusion budget, the de-escalation-bonus structure admits a reward mis-specification. Under the original naive contract (\texttt{impact\_is\_terminal}${}={}$\textit{True}) and an unbounded attacker, agents earn a large terminal survival bonus ($+250$) plus per-step proportionality and de-escalation bonuses; this sum dwarfs the expected loss at the \textsc{impact} step. The reward-maximising policy is therefore ``rack up de-escalation bonuses and accept the \textsc{impact} loss'' --- a divergence from the intended policy of ``defend the \textsc{impact} step''. This is a textbook case of reward mis-specification \cite{Sutton2018,Krakovna2020}: the proxy objective is correctly optimised in a way that diverges from the human's intended objective. Two complementary fixes are adopted in this dissertation. First, the reward-component ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}) identifies a structural reward fix --- setting \texttt{impact\_is\_terminal}${}={}$\textit{False}, which turns the \textsc{impact} row into an actionable decision step rather than an immediate terminal penalty. Second, and more fundamentally, the \emph{finite intrusion budget} (Section~\ref{ssec:intrusion_budget}) makes \emph{prevention} an achievable outcome: an attacker that exhausts its budget of $40$ progression steps before reaching \textsc{impact} is stopped outright. Under the finite-budget contract the compromise rate is no longer pinned at $1.0$ but varies by policy (e.g.\ \BestAgentName{} $0.40$, always-\textsc{block} $0.00$ and the oracle $0.00$, both of which fully prevent), so prevention --- not merely post-hoc mitigation --- becomes a measurable axis of the held-out benchmark.

RF-Acting achieves higher mean reward ($+\RFReward$ vs.\ \BestAgentName{} $+\BestAgentReward$, $\Delta = +169.7$) at the cost of \RFLatency\,ms p50 inference latency --- a \textbf{\LatencyRatio$\times$ latency penalty} relative to trained \BestAgentName{} (\BestAgentLatency\,ms p50) under the measured single-sample, unbatched, Python/scikit-learn dispatch regime on the evaluation host. \paragraph{Benign false-positive rate (an operational constraint, satisfied).} All three trained DRL agents keep their benign-traffic false-positive rate (the fraction of true \textsc{benign} steps on which they choose \textsc{block} or \textsc{isolate}) \emph{below} the 1\,\% operational threshold: DQN \textbf{\FPRdqnPct\,\%}, A2C \textbf{\FPRatcPct\,\%}, PPO \textbf{\FPRppoPct\,\%}. Under the tug-of-war dynamics a correct response to a \textsc{benign} stage is the low-force \textsc{observe}/\textsc{log} action, and the capped proportionality and de-escalation bonuses remove any incentive to over-apply \textsc{block}/\textsc{isolate} on benign traffic. The three agents are statistically indistinguishable on this metric, and none exhibits the high-reward-implies-high-FPR coupling reported in the pre-redesign environment. The trivial \textsc{always-block} baseline, by contrast, has a 100\,\% benign FPR, which is precisely why it is the worst-scoring policy on reward despite preventing every compromise.

\paragraph{Proportionality on non-IMPACT stages (G6.3 PASS).} The F6 confusion matrices (Figure~\ref{fig:f6}) show a clear diagonal structure: \textsc{benign} $\to$ mostly \textsc{observe}/\textsc{log}; \textsc{access} $\to$ \textsc{restrict}; \textsc{maneuver} $\to$ \textsc{block}. All three trained-RL policies clear the 0.70 proportionality threshold on all non-\textsc{impact} stages (DQN 0.867, PPO 0.893, A2C 0.940). RL training successfully internalised the kill-chain recommended-action structure for every non-\textsc{impact} stage; the \textsc{impact} deficit is the perception limit under partial observability, examined in Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}.

\paragraph{\textsc{restrict} is a first-class proportional action.} Under the tug-of-war dynamics, \textsc{restrict} is the recommended (proportional) response at the \textsc{access} stage: choosing it both earns the proportionality bonus \emph{and} de-escalates the attacker one stage with probability $p_{\text{down}}=0.90$ while draining the intrusion budget, exactly as \textsc{block} does at \textsc{maneuver} and \textsc{isolate} does at \textsc{impact} (with \textsc{isolate} strictly stronger, $p_{\text{down}}^{\textsc{isolate}}=0.98$). \textsc{restrict} is therefore neither inert nor dominated: applying a stronger action than \textsc{access} warrants (\textsc{block}/\textsc{isolate}) merely \emph{holds} the attacker rather than reversing it, and is additionally penalised as disproportionate over-force on benign-adjacent traffic. The confusion-matrix mass on \textsc{access}$\to$\textsc{restrict} thus reflects an operationally useful, force-proportionate mitigation, and the graded force ladder (\textsc{restrict} $<$ \textsc{block} $<$ \textsc{isolate}) is fully exercised by the trained policies.

\paragraph{F9 --- structural fix identifies the contract design defect (G7.2 PASS-WITH-FINDING).} Across the 12-cell sparse one-at-a-time sweep, no reward-coefficient cell moves the reward above the primary benchmark \BestAgentName{} baseline ($+\BestAgentReward$) by $\geq 1\sigma$; coefficient tuning alone does not close the raw-reward gap, which activates finding \texttt{D7.1.1}. The structural \texttt{impact\_is\_terminal}${}={}$\textit{False} cell, in contrast, lifts mean test reward to $+\FnineStructuralReward$ and raises the diagnostic mitigated-impact rate to $\FnineStructuralMitRate$, versus $0.0$ for the mis-specified baseline \emph{cell} measured in this same unbounded reward-ablation strand (distinct from the budgeted deployable-best policy's in-benchmark mitigated-impact rate of $0.123$, reported in Section~\ref{sec:benchmark_results}). This is the F9 \emph{mitigation-diagnostic strand}: while raw reward does not clear the stretch threshold, the post-impact mitigation diagnostic improves by more than $5\times$ under the corrected contract. This directional finding motivated the primary contract choice (\texttt{impact\_is\_terminal}${}={}$\textit{False}) for the full \NumSeeds{}-seed benchmark, where prevention rate is the primary defender-attributable security KPI.

The mechanism is interpretable: under the naive contract \texttt{impact\_is\_terminal}${}={}$\textit{True}, an \textsc{impact} transition immediately ends the episode with a fixed terminal penalty; the agent has at most one chance to defend and the de-escalation bonuses earned earlier dominate the expected \textsc{impact} loss. Under the corrected primary contract \texttt{impact\_is\_terminal}${}={}$\textit{False}, the \textsc{impact} row becomes one more decision step, and the agent can earn the $+b_{\mathrm{success}}$ bonus by choosing \textsc{block}/\textsc{isolate} \emph{during} \textsc{impact}. This resolves the mis-specification at its source: the intended objective (defend the \textsc{impact} step) is now structurally achievable under the reward function. The original \texttt{impact\_is\_terminal}${}={}$\textit{True} contract is retained in this dissertation as a \emph{reward mis-specification case study} (the case-study results are summarised in the ablation; the primary benchmark uses \texttt{impact\_is\_terminal}${}={}$\textit{False} throughout).

\paragraph{F9 caveat: the reward ablation isolates mitigation, the budget enables prevention.} The F9 reward-component ablation runs on the unbounded attacker (no intrusion budget), so prevention is unavailable and compromise remains near-total across cells (observed \texttt{compromise\_rate} range $0.89$--$0.993$). Within that unbounded strand the F9 win must be read as ``post-\textsc{impact} mitigation'' --- the agent lets one \textsc{impact} row happen, then defends it during the now-non-terminal step --- and the elevated mitigated-impact rate ($\FnineStructuralMitRate$) is the meaningful diagnostic. The complementary axis is the \emph{finite intrusion budget} of the primary benchmark (Section~\ref{ssec:intrusion_budget}), under which \texttt{compromise\_rate} drops below $1.0$ and pre-\textsc{impact} prevention becomes measurable. The two are deliberately disjoint: F9 isolates the reward-shaping effect with the budget held off; the headline benchmark isolates the prevention effect with the budget on.

\paragraph{The detector-independence dividend: RL preserves reward on the supervised blind spot.} On \texttt{VulnerabilityScan} --- the class on which the upstream supervised stage detector is essentially blind (recall $0.001$, Section~\ref{sec:detector_results}) --- the detector-free trained agents do not merely survive: they \emph{decisively outperform} the detector-coupled RF-Acting baseline on reward. PPO reaches $+298.3$ (CI $[275.5, 320.0]$), within seed-noise of its in-distribution mean ($+\BestAgentReward$), whereas RF-Acting collapses to $-4430.6$ (CI $[-4472.4, -4389.0]$); the gap is $\Delta = +4728.9$ at $\geq 1\sigma$. The win is one of reward and mitigation robustness rather than prevention: under the corrected non-terminal contract PPO still reaches \textsc{impact} in most episodes (compromise rate $0.98$) but mitigates the impact step well enough to stay net-positive, while RF-Acting's classifier-induced action stream is systematically mismatched to the OOD feature distribution and bleeds penalty over full-length episodes. Gate~G7.9 \emph{passes}: the pre-registered finding-activation rule that would have narrowed the claim to a limitation does \emph{not} fire, and the OOD result is promoted to a Tier-1 deliverable (audit finding AF1).

\begin{quote}\itshape
The detector-coupled baseline carries a catastrophic out-of-distribution failure mode that the detector-free learned policy does not. On the one held-out class where the upstream stage detector is structurally blind (recall $0.001$), the baseline that scored highest in-distribution ($+\RFReward$) collapses by nearly five thousand reward points, while the trained DRL policy --- which never consults a classifier --- preserves near-in-distribution reward. This is the sharpest single demonstration of the central thesis: the deployment value of acting directly on raw feature rows is not raw inference speed but the elimination of a brittle perception dependency.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Why RF-Acting's reward collapses while the learned policy stays net-positive.} The mechanism is a consequence of the tug-of-war dynamics interacting with a blind upstream classifier, and the telemetry makes the failure mode precise. On \texttt{VulnerabilityScan} the stage detector mis-predicts the \textsc{recon} stage (recall $0.001$), so RF-Acting selects systematically mismatched actions throughout the episode. Crucially, this does \emph{not} manifest as a high compromise rate: RF-Acting records compromise rate $0.0$ on this class and runs to the full $100$-step horizon, accumulating per-step proportionality, over-force, and availability penalties that compound into a reward of $-4430.6$ over a long episode. The failure is therefore a sustained mis-proportional-action penalty collapse, not a run-to-\textsc{impact} collapse. The trained DRL agents, having learned a force-proportionate policy directly from feature statistics rather than from a stage label, choose actions that remain reward-sensible even on unseen features: they still reach \textsc{impact} in most episodes (compromise rate $0.98$) but mitigate it under the corrected non-terminal contract (mitigated-impact rate $0.81$), retaining net-positive reward ($+298.3$). The result inverts the usual supervised-versus-RL intuition: it is precisely the supervised baseline's dependence on a fallible classifier that makes it fragile under distribution shift, and the detector-free RL policy that preserves reward robustly. The win is thus one of reward and mitigation robustness under distribution shift rather than of prevention. Generalising this robustness to a broader family of OOD classes through train-time OOD-augmented data is identified as future work (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}).

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}\label{sec:discussion}
The headline finding of this dissertation is that, on real CICIoT2023 feature rows sampled into a simulated five-stage kill-chain trajectory, model-free DRL agents that \emph{never observe the true kill-chain stage} --- acting on raw feature rows alone, a partially observable (POMDP) control task --- \emph{capture \OracleCapturePct\,\% of the value of perfect stage detection} on a held-out test split and dominate every non-RL trivial baseline with non-overlapping confidence intervals, while keeping their benign false-positive rate below $1\,\%$. The interior placement of the learned policies, relative to the stage-aware oracle that attains perfect security at near-zero availability cost, \emph{quantifies the cost of partial observability} (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}, F12). The comparison against the strongest deployable supervised baseline (RF-Acting, $+\RFReward$) is an honest deployment trade-off, not a reward-domination claim: RF-Acting attains a higher reward in-distribution but depends on an upstream stage classifier and incurs a \RFLatency\,ms per-decision latency, whereas the RL agents are detector-free and sub-$0.1\,$ms per decision. That detector dependency is not free: on the one held-out class where the upstream stage detector is blind (\texttt{VulnerabilityScan}), RF-Acting's reward collapses by nearly five thousand points while the detector-free RL policy stays net-positive (Section~\ref{sec:ood_results}, F15). This is a reward-robustness win rather than a prevention win --- the RL policy still reaches \textsc{impact} in most episodes but mitigates it under the corrected contract, whereas RF-Acting bleeds per-step penalties across the full horizon --- but it remains the sharpest evidence that the deployment value of acting on raw feature rows is the elimination of a brittle perception dependency, not merely inference speed. The reward mis-specification identified in the naive contract (\texttt{impact\_is\_terminal}${}={}$\textit{True}) is understood and structurally characterised; the corrected contract (\texttt{impact\_is\_terminal}${}={}$\textit{False}) combined with a \emph{finite intrusion budget} is the primary evaluation contract. Under that finite-budget contract, prevention is an achievable and measured outcome --- the calibrated budget makes prevention defender-attributable (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}, F16) --- though it remains contingent on that budget. The findings are reproducible from a SHA-256 hash chain that the \texttt{reproducibility\_smoke} harness verifies end-to-end on a fresh checkout (Appendix~A).

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}\label{sec:threats_to_validity} (1) \textbf{Designed attacker dynamics.} The attacker is a designed tug-of-war process: at each active stage it reacts to the defender's action --- a proportionate response pushes it one stage back the kill chain (with probability $p_{\text{down}}$), an under-forced response lets it advance, and an over-forced response merely holds it --- under a finite intrusion budget. Its rule set and budget are chosen by the experimenter, not learned or adversarially co-trained. The prevention results therefore hold against \emph{this} reactive-but-not-co-adapting adversary; a jointly trained, co-adapting attacker is future work. The evasion-before-commit robustness check (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}, F17) shows the learned defense degrades gracefully when the attacker stalls in response to forced blocks. (2) \textbf{MTTC floor.} Mean MTTC values ($\sim$24--25) are bounded by the lifecycle-floor \textsc{impact} clamp and should be read as ``lifecycle-floor-bounded'' rather than ``unconstrained natural'' MTTC. (3) \textbf{Benign FPR.} Benign FPR is below the $1\,\%$ operational threshold for all three trained agents (\FPRdqnPct--\FPRppoPct\,\%); the pre-redesign coupling in which the best-reward agent carried the highest false-alarm cost is eliminated, because the capped proportionality and de-escalation bonuses remove any incentive to over-block benign traffic. (4) \textbf{Deployable comparison.} The trained agents do \emph{not} beat RF-Acting on the in-distribution \texttt{test\_balanced} split, where RF-Acting attains the higher reward; the deployable comparison there is a latency--reward trade-off frontier. This ordering does \emph{not} hold under distribution shift: on the \texttt{VulnerabilityScan} OOD class, where the upstream stage detector is blind, the detector-free RL policy reverses the reward ordering by a large margin (a reward-robustness win via mitigation, not a prevention win; Section~\ref{sec:ood_results}, F15). The oracle rule is a measurement instrument, not a competing baseline. (5) \textbf{Force-gradient calibration.} The graded ladder \textsc{restrict}${}<{}$\textsc{block}${}<{}$\textsc{isolate} is fully exercised (\textsc{isolate} de-escalates with probability $0.98$ versus $0.90$ for \textsc{block}), so no defensive action is dominated; the residual design sensitivity is the choice of these de-escalation probabilities, which the environment-difficulty sweep (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}, F10) probes. (6) \textbf{Simulator fidelity.} Features are sampled i.i.d.\ from per-stage rows of CICIoT2023 (the 29-feature vector whose provenance is documented in Appendix~B and \texttt{docs/results/dataset/feature\_provenance.json}); temporal and cross-flow correlations within a real session are not preserved. (7) \textbf{Statistical power.} With $n = \NumSeeds{}$ seeds the Welch $t$-test has limited power to distinguish among the three DRL algorithms, which is why their 95\,\% CIs overlap substantially.
